## Artefact 1: Understanding BPMN Symbols and Structure

Selected artefact: Khantong and Sriboonlue (2026), ‘Robotic Process Automation in Business Process Management: A Systematic Literature Review and an Integrated Framework’

Khantong and Sriboonlue (2026) examine how Robotic Process Automation is used within Business Process Management. Their review identifies six important areas, including process selection, implementation, performance benefits, challenges, technology integration and organisational governance. The authors combine these areas into a framework based on strategic alignment, operational execution and continuous improvement. This shows that RPA should be managed as part of a wider BPM approach rather than treated as an isolated software tool (Khantong & Sriboonlue, 2026, p. 1).

Before reading this article, I understood RPA mainly as software bots taking over repetitive work. What stood out to me was that automation is only one part of the picture. Organisations also need to choose suitable processes, manage implementation and keep checking whether the automated process is performing as intended. The framework helped me connect RPA with the wider BPM lifecycle instead of viewing it as a separate software tool.

## Artefact 2: Organisational Factors Affecting RPA Adoption

Selected artefact: Durão and Palma dos Reis (2025), ‘An Organizational Perspective on Robotic Process Automation Adoption and Usage Factors’

Durão and Palma dos Reis (2025) investigate why organisations decide to adopt and use RPA. Their mixed-method study combines interviews with survey data from 141 organisations. The findings show that compatibility and relative advantage are significant technological factors. Organisations are therefore more likely to adopt RPA when it fits their existing systems and procedures and when decision-makers recognise clear benefits from its use (Durão & Palma dos Reis, 2025, p. 1).

This study made me question my earlier assumption that organisations adopt RPA mainly because it saves time. The findings showed me that a tool may offer useful features but still be unsuitable if it does not work well with existing systems and procedures. I found this important because it links technology decisions with process planning. Before automating, an organisation needs to understand the process and decide whether RPA will provide a practical advantage.

## Artefact 3: Cybersecurity Threats in RPA Systems

Selected artefact: Kandula, Kassetty and Mogulluri (2025), ‘Towards a Secure Robotic Process Automation Ecosystem: Threats and Countermeasures’

Kandula, Kassetty and Mogulluri (2025) identify cybersecurity threats affecting RPA systems. These include hard-coded credentials, excessive bot privileges, weak access controls, insider misuse, insecure workflows and vulnerable system integrations. Because bots may access several applications and sensitive records, a compromised or incorrectly configured bot can expose information or perform unauthorised actions. The authors show that efficiency benefits can create serious risks when security is not considered during automation design (Kandula, Kassetty & Mogulluri, 2025, p. 7).

The part that stood out to me was how much access a software bot may have. I had mainly considered benefits such as faster work and fewer manual errors without thinking carefully about what could happen if a bot’s credentials were stolen. I now see that an over-privileged or poorly configured bot can create serious consequences. Cybersecurity therefore needs to be considered while designing the process, not added only after the bot has been deployed.

## Artefact 4: Security Governance for RPA

Selected artefact: North Carolina Department of Health and Human Services (2025), ‘Policy NCDHHS Robotic Process Automation’

The North Carolina Department of Health and Human Services (2025) provides practical security requirements for managing RPA platforms and bots. The policy requires authorised administration, software patching, multifactor authentication, protected audit logs and monitoring of platform activity. It also requires each robot to have a unique non-human identity and access only the functions that have been documented, tested and approved. These controls create accountability throughout the automated process (North Carolina Department of Health and Human Services, 2025, p. 2).

I found this policy useful because it turns general security ideas into actions an organisation can actually follow. The requirement for every bot to have its own identity was particularly meaningful to me because it makes individual bot activity easier to trace. It also showed me that responsibility does not end when automation begins. Organisations must continue reviewing access, behaviour, system changes and logs throughout the bot’s lifecycle to keep the process controlled and secure.

## Reflection and Conclusion

These artefacts changed the way I think about RPA. At first, I focused mostly on its ability to complete repetitive work quickly. I now understand that an organisation must also consider whether the process is suitable, whether the technology fits its existing systems and whether the expected benefit is clear. The cybersecurity sources were especially useful because they showed that bots may have access to sensitive information and several applications. I would now evaluate an automated process by asking not only whether it is efficient, but also who controls the bot, what access it has and how its activity will be monitored.
